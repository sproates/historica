Kerma was the capital of a Nubian kingdom near the Nile’s Third Cataract. Its city and eastern cemetery preserve evidence spanning roughly 2500–1500 BCE. Houses, stores and workshops formed part of a settlement whose religious quarter was dominated by the large mudbrick Western Deffufa.

Livestock keeping, plant resources, hunting and fishing supported life there. Trade in cattle, gold, ivory and other goods also contributed to its economy. Kerma needs to be understood as a society in its own right, alongside the history of Egypt to the north.

One smaller connection is particularly tangible: a Kerma beaker found in a tomb at Abydos in Egypt. It tells us something about the movement of objects, although it cannot by itself establish the identity of its owner.
